## The 9 questions, answered

### 1. What role should Angelina, Bella and Scarlett play?

**Corrected 2026-08-15, per Katie:** not a fixed rule that the girls are "the creator, not the character." That understates a real WAG asset. **Governing principle instead: build WAG-owned IP capable of surviving independently of the girls, while intelligently leveraging the girls' existing personalities, audience, and credibility wherever doing so increases product value, acquisition, fandom, or conversion.** Their voices, personalities, real photos/video, challenges, QR experiences, introductions, and humor are all real, usable participation channels — which ones actually improve a given product is an empirical question Publishing determines per-product, not a rule decided in the abstract here. The proven sample spreads already use real transcript-sourced banter (a real participation channel that works); whether photo/video/direct-address participation adds more value on top of that is untested, not ruled out.

### 2. Do original recurring character(s) materially improve the strategy?

**Yes, with a specific, evidenced reason: durability.** Real precedent found in this research, directly on point: Bluey's creator (Joe Brumm) stepped back from day-to-day show involvement in December 2024 and the show continues; Peppa Pig's entire founding creative team exited production in 2021 and the franchise kept growing; Daniel Tiger's Neighborhood was deliberately *created nine years after* Fred Rogers' death specifically to extend his legacy. A character system that doesn't depend on any one person's continuous involvement is the proven pattern for a franchise built to outlast its origin circumstances — directly relevant to WAG's real situation (3 real teenagers who will grow up, and whose involvement in WAG itself may change over years).

### 3. What do those characters need to accomplish, narratively and commercially?

**Narratively:** carry a consistent voice/personality independent of any single book's subject matter (already true of Wobbie's locked design and behavioral rule — reacts, never narrates). **Commercially:** be ownable outright by WAG (see Rights, below), visually distinctive enough to work at both full-page and thumbnail scale, and — per Katie's own test from the original Publishing directive — wanted "even when a kid isn't doing a slime experiment." That last bar is unproven; it's exactly what the observation test's Wobbie-attachment dimension is designed to check.

### 4. One mascot, multiple characters, or another structure?

**Real precedent splits cleanly by what worked:** M&M's and PAW Patrol succeeded with an *ensemble* (multiple distinct personalities in relationship to each other); Paddington, Peter Rabbit, Winnie the Pooh, Very Hungry Caterpillar all succeeded as a *single* character. The single-character pattern is lower-complexity and matches what WAG already has (Wobbie, alone, already locked). **Recommendation, corrected per Katie: start prototyping with Wobbie present, but his centrality to the franchise must be earned through actual audience/customer response, not presumed because he's the most-developed asset today.** Whether he ends up carrying every future property, one strong branch, or gets rethought entirely is a real open question the observation test and Book #1's real reception answer — not decided here. Equally, "does WAG need a second character" stays open in both directions — not assumed necessary, not ruled out. The M&M's ensemble pattern is real and works elsewhere, but it's the highest-investment path — not the right first bet before Book #1 proves anything.

### 5. How does the character/IP travel across Slime → Science → Animals → Experiments → Adventures?

**This is the single most important finding from this research pass, and it changes the recommendation.** Real precedent for "a mascot invented specifically to hop between otherwise-unrelated topic categories" is genuinely rare and weakly evidenced. What actually works, per real examples found:
- **A travel-device premise where the variety IS the story world** (Magic School Bus, Magic Tree House) — the strongest working pattern found.
- **Borrowing an already-famous character's existing equity into new topics after the fact** (Curious George Discovers, Cat in the Hat's Learning Library) — requires the character to already be beloved first.
- **No character at all — pure graphic/editorial brand consistency** (National Geographic Kids, DK Eyewitness — arguably the two biggest real cross-topic kids'-nonfiction brands in the world).

**Implication for WAG:** don't position Wobbie as a generic host mascot pasted onto separate future subject books. Position **"WAG Lab" itself as the story world** — the place Wobbie and the girls occupy — with Wobbie as its resident companion. This is structurally closer to the Magic School Bus mechanism (the world/premise travels, carrying its cast with it) than to "corporate mascot license extended onto unrelated nonfiction," which has no strong success precedent. Practically: future books become "WAG Science Lab," "WAG Animal Lab," etc. — the same *place*, a new subject — rather than "Wobbie visits a science book."

### 6. What makes it recognizable, emotionally appealing, and ownable?

**Recognizable:** Wobbie's already-locked visual signature (goggles, sewn patches, huggable proportions) independent of any single page — exactly the mechanism every successful character studied shares. **Emotionally appealing:** the real, evidenced pattern is a consistent personality with genuine (if light) flaws played for warmth, not perfection — Wobbie's established "believes in himself more than his size justifies" trait already fits this. **Ownable:** requires the actual legal work in the Rights section below — recognizability alone doesn't create ownership.

### 7. How could it extend into plush, merchandise, animation/video, games, collectibles, licensing?

Real precedent confirms the standard sequence: book/show first, licensing program follows once real audience affection exists — Peter Rabbit is the fastest documented case (~1 year from book to Potter's own hand-made plush prototype); most others took years to decades. **Do not front-load merchandise investment before Book #1 proves real reader attachment** — consistent with Katie's own standing instruction not to overbuild ahead of evidence. Real rights note from this research: licensing programs typically split publishing rights, merchandising rights, and media rights into separate agreements/agents (confirmed via real, current examples: Sesame Street, Yo Gabba Gabba!, Hello Kitty) — WAG doesn't need to solve all three now, just needs to not accidentally sign away any one of them prematurely.

### 8. How do recurring codes, missions, badges, discoveries and digital experiences create collectibility?

Real precedent found: **Where's Waldo** (hidden-character search, 13M+ copies across the first 4 books) and **Hidden Mickeys** (Disney's real, deliberately-planted find-the-mascot mechanic, running continuously since the late 1980s) are the strongest real analogues to WAG's existing "find Wobbie on the page" mechanic and the proposed Secret Code system — both are proven, both are exactly the same underlying idea WAG already has in draft form. **Honest gap found:** no strong real example exists of a badge/rank system built around a single proprietary *character* specifically (Accelerated Reader and Scholastic's reading-challenge badges are real and work, but use generic iconography, not deep character lore) — meaning Format B/D's Lab Rank system, if it works in testing, would be a genuinely novel combination, not a copy of a proven exact mechanic. That's a real opportunity, evidenced by absence rather than by a direct comp.

### 9. How do the girls stay central, not replaced by generic fictional characters?

By construction, not by hoping: their real voice (transcript-sourced banter, not invented dialogue) stays the differentiator no competitor can copy — this was already established as WAG's real moat in the original franchise-format package. Wobbie doesn't replace them; he's a companion inside a world *they* built. If "WAG Lab" becomes the throughline (per #5), the girls remain its real, on-page hosts — the place travels because they're the ones taking the reader there, the same structural role Ms. Frizzle plays in Magic School Bus's story world, just filled by 3 real people instead of one invented teacher.

## Real rights work required before any commissioning — flagged to Legal/Rights, not resolved here

**This section, and the accompanying research PDF, are a research memo — not legal clearance.** General industry-practice information only, gathered by web research, not by counsel. Nothing here should be treated as WAG having cleared the Wobbie name, secured any rights, or received actual legal advice — Legal/Rights and real counsel still need to do the actual clearance and drafting work before any name is used publicly or any illustrator is commissioned.
- **"Work for hire" checkbox in a boilerplate contract is likely legally ineffective** for a standalone commissioned character illustration — it only automatically applies to 9 specific statutory categories under 17 U.S.C. §101, and a mascot design isn't one of them. The correct instrument is an explicit **written copyright assignment**, covering the character concept itself (not just delivered artwork), its name, and derivative/future-media rights.
- **Whichever illustrator eventually designs the final character, WAG must obtain full copyright assignment plus explicit future-media/derivative/merchandise rights from them** — not just a license to use one set of delivered drawings. This is the single most important rights item before any commission proceeds.
- Publishing rights, merchandising rights, and media rights are conventionally split into separate agreements — WAG should decide now only what it needs for Book #1 (publishing), not pre-negotiate the others.
- Real, documented enforcement precedent exists for exactly WAG's kind of risk: Hasbro/eOne won both a UK copyright case and a Shanghai trademark case against a Peppa Pig knockoff ("Wolfoo") — confirms this is a real, litigated risk category, not theoretical.
- The "Wobbie" name collision (real published "Wobbie the Wasp" character) remains unresolved and should be cleared before any public commitment to the name.
