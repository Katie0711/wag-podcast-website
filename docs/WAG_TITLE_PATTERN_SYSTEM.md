## The core rule — PREMISE FIRST, FORMULA SECOND

**[HYPOTHESIS, Katie-approved as a permanent principle, 2026-08-16]**

Never force a successful title formula onto a video where the underlying mechanism doesn't genuinely exist in the footage. A formula is a lens for finding the strongest *truthful* story already inside a video — not a template to fill in.

Five permanently approved sub-principles (Katie's exact framing, 2026-08-16):
1. Premise first. Formula second.
2. Pattern fit > pattern popularity.
3. Premise > wording.
4. Clarity + curiosity > cleverness.
5. Truthful story > manufactured stakes.

**Existing footage vs. pre-production.** For existing footage: find the strongest story that *actually happened* — never invent escalation, danger, secrets, competition, or stakes to fit a formula. For pre-production: the video can be *designed* around a stronger structure before filming (e.g., deliberately building in an "every time X, Y gets harder" escalation) — that's legitimate, because nothing is being manufactured after the fact.

**A-story / B-story guardrail** `[HYPOTHESIS, Katie-corrected, 2026-08-16]` — this is a distinct failure mode from pattern-forcing, and it is just as real. The clearest, most *structured* mechanic in a transcript (points, teams, a stated winner) is not automatically the dominant viewer experience. A title must represent the A-story — what the video is actually about for most of its runtime — unless a secondary story is genuinely strong and pervasive enough that packaging around it would not misrepresent what the viewer receives. A B-story with cleaner stakes does not automatically become the title just because it's easier to verify and easier to write a formula against. Determine the A-story by watching/reading the whole video's balance of attention and time, not by which thread has the most quotable, structured evidence.

**Transcript ≠ story** `[HYPOTHESIS, Katie-corrected, 2026-08-16]` — the single most important standing correction in this document. An auto-caption transcript surfaces explicit, nameable mechanics extremely well: stated points, named teams, declared winners, direct quotes. It does NOT surface the dominant entertainment experience equally well — real things like a park/setting itself, girls' authentic reactions, personality, recurring relationships with other real people, and emotional arcs (excitement, fear, a bittersweet goodbye) are all real story content that a transcript renders as flat, easy-to-miss lines next to a competition's crisp, quotable structure. The correct process is:

**FOOTAGE → A-story → strongest truthful premise → psychological framing → title/thumbnail**

not:

**FOOTAGE → find the clearest mechanic → title it.**

Before finalizing any title, explicitly ask what the video is about for *most* of its runtime — not just which thread produced the most extractable transcript evidence.

## The real failure case that produced this doc

**[WAG]** Disney Day 2 (`pMcPR0u-_qI`, published 2026-08-14). The first title pass tried forcing high-performing external formulas ("ranking," "hype-test," "survival," "escalation") onto the video without checking the actual footage. Real verification (auto-caption transcript, read start-to-finish, plus a direct visual check of the true ending) proved:
- No ranking or "best ride" verdict exists anywhere in the video, spoken or visual.
- No genuine escalation ("rides get scarier") exists.
- The real, verified story is a team competition (real placements/points stated on camera) with a real but lower-key consequence bit — not a dramatic "survival" or "punishment wheel" moment strong enough to confirm as a title anchor.

**Lesson, now permanent:** a title formula that performed well elsewhere is worthless if the specific video doesn't actually contain the mechanism that formula depends on. Verify the actual story before pattern-matching, every time — not just when something feels off.

**Second, distinct lesson from the same video, corrected 2026-08-16:** after verifying the ranking/hype premise was false, the first re-analysis pass found a real, cleanly-structured team competition (points, placements, a stated loser) in the transcript and promoted it directly into "the actual story" — without checking whether it was actually the dominant viewer experience across the video's runtime, or a B-story running underneath something else. Katie's correction: it's a B-story. The real A-story, watched as a whole, is closer to a day-in-the-life/farewell vlog of the trip's last day — real named attractions, real recurring special guests (the "Disney Insiders"), the girls' authentic reactions (fear of heights, disorientation, excitement), shopping, snack-tasting, and a bittersweet goodbye — with the competition threaded through it as a recurring bit, not the throughline. This is exactly what the Transcript ≠ Story principle above exists to prevent, and it happened on the very first attempt to apply that principle — proof the guardrail needs to be explicit, not assumed.

## The 12 patterns (condensed, provenance-tagged)

For each: the psychological mechanism, when it's legitimate, and the trap.

1. **EVERY / COMPLETION** `[EXTERNAL]` — scale + definitiveness. Legit when completion itself is genuinely the interesting thing. Trap: describing an activity, not a mission.
2. **COMPLETION + CONSTRAINT** `[EXTERNAL]` — adds a real limitation (`IN ONE DAY`, `BEFORE TIME RUNS OUT`, `WITH $X`). `[WAG]` This is the real Video 1 formula: *"I Rode EVERY Disney EXTREME Ride in ONE DAY!"* Confirmed working structure per the real filename convention ("Disney Day 1/Day 2").
3. **ATTEMPT > REVEALED SUCCESS** `[EXTERNAL]` — "we tried" preserves outcome uncertainty; "we did" gives it away. Only preserve uncertainty that's real — don't manufacture doubt where the video shows unambiguous success.
4. **ESCALATION** `[EXTERNAL]` — "Every time X, Y gets harder." Powerful because it drives both CTR and retention architecture (viewer expects the next beat to top the last). **Highest-risk pattern for false-fit** — only use when the footage shows genuine progression. Confirmed absent in the Disney Day 2 case; do not force it again without direct evidence.
5. **SECRET / INFORMATION ASYMMETRY** `[EXTERNAL]` — one party knows something another doesn't. Only legitimate when real secrecy exists in the footage.
6. **SOMETHING IS WRONG** `[EXTERNAL]` — a stated problem with a withheld explanation ("Someone Stole My Jeep"). Starts a story rather than describing an activity.
7. **ONE IS DIFFERENT** `[EXTERNAL]` — identity gap ("ONE Gymnast is EVIL"). Viewer asks "which one?"
8. **LEVELS** `[EXTERNAL]` — progression + completion combined. Only if the content can legitimately be organized as levels.
9. **RELATIONSHIP STAKES** `[EXTERNAL]` — the people, not the activity, carry the stakes. `[HYPOTHESIS]` Especially relevant for WAG — the girls' real personalities/dynamic should be doing packaging work, not just their activity. Always ask: is there a stronger human story inside this activity than the activity itself?
10. **DISCOVERY / FOUND** `[EXTERNAL]` — "I Found [unexpected thing]." Only if something is genuinely found.
11. **SURVIVAL** `[EXTERNAL]` — legitimate only when the subject genuinely implies adversity. "Surviving Detention" — fine. "Surviving Disney" — forced, per the real Disney Day 2 test; Disney doesn't naturally require survival.
12. **QUESTION / OUTCOME** `[EXTERNAL]` — converts the mission into one unresolved question the whole video answers. Can create a visible progression mechanic (3 → 7 → 12 → final count). Only use when genuine uncertainty exists.

**Rejected pattern for Disney Day 2, with reasoning:** RANKING/BEST/HYPE — real transcript + visual verification proved this story isn't in the footage. Documented as the canonical example of pattern-forcing to avoid repeating.

## The one-question test

For every candidate title: what ONE question does this put in the viewer's head? If there's no obvious answer, the title is likely describing the video rather than telling its story.

## Title + thumbnail division of labor

**[EXTERNAL, WAG-adopted]** Title and thumbnail must carry *different* information — never let thumbnail text repeat what the title already says. The title communicates mission/scale/constraint/novelty; the thumbnail communicates the visual/emotional stakes. When redesigning a thumbnail is on the table (not locked to the current asset), decide the *whole composition*, not just the text overlay — ask whether teams/consequence/personality should be visually exposed instead of a generic reaction shot.

## Every-bucket test

Before selecting a title, run the real premise through every plausible bucket (completion, constraint, attempt, levels, ranking, discovery, competition, relationship, question/outcome, escalation-only-if-real). Reject every bucket the footage doesn't truthfully support. Do not generate 20 wording variations of the same bucket — that's Mad-Libs, not analysis.

## Scoring dimensions

Premise strength, clarity, curiosity, scale, emotional stakes, constraint, novelty, character/personality potential, thumbnail potential, title-thumbnail complement, retention architecture, payoff, truthfulness — plus WAG-specific additions: cold-viewer accessibility, differentiation from the paired/adjacent video, WAG-brand fit, repeatable-format potential. These are comparative reasoning aids, not precise measurements — never fake false numerical precision.

## Anti-patterns (reject on sight)

Generic activity description ("We Go to Disney") · generic challenge wording ("Disney Ride Challenge") · forced danger ("Surviving Disney") · fake escalation the footage doesn't show · unnatural superlatives the experience doesn't naturally earn · empty hype ("You Won't Believe...") · title/thumbnail duplication · giving away the outcome unnecessarily · copying a competitor's exact execution instead of learning the underlying structure · Mad-Libs adjective-swapping (EXTREME → NEW → POPULAR → VIRAL → HYPED) presented as if it were real differentiation.

## Output discipline

Do the broad pattern analysis internally. Return only: actual video story (one sentence), strongest story mechanism (one sentence), #1 best title, #2 challenger (genuinely different psychological approach, not a wording variant), #3 wild card (experimental but defensible), then a direct keep/change call. Never a 20-title brainstorm. Never flatter the existing title to seem useful. If the original is strongest, say so — if the premise itself is weak, say so.

### Pattern: Completion + Constraint
- **Example:** *"I Rode EVERY Disney EXTREME Ride in ONE DAY!"*
- **Evidence source:** WAG (real, live)
- **Sample size:** 1 WAG video with full lifecycle data as of this edition
- **Where it worked:** Real Browse-features testing occurred (10.7K impressions, 70% of all impressions) — the package got a genuine algorithmic test.
- **Where it failed:** CTR on Browse specifically was weak (1.7%); severe early retention drop-off (44.8% of typical at the first second, down to 14–16% through the middle).
- **Likely reason:** UNKNOWN whether the weak Browse CTR is a packaging problem or the retention collapse is suppressing further amplification independent of packaging — not yet separable with current evidence.
- **Best suited to:** UNKNOWN pending more samples.
- **Known failure modes:** Two videos in this format back-to-back ("Day 1"/"Day 2") both defaulting to the same completion-formula title reads as one video split in half, not two reasons to watch.
- **Confidence:** LOW (n=1).
- **Last updated:** 2026-08-16.
