### 1. The Book of Slime

- **Title / author-publisher:** *The Book of Slime — A Kid's Guide on How to Make the Best Slimes of All Time*; credited to "Peanut Prodigy," functioning as both author and imprint — reads as a self-published/KDP-pattern title, not a traditional publishing house.
- **Details:** pub. 2020-10-19; ISBN-13 9781649430335; $13.99 (Walmart, confirmed); paperback, ~48–50 pages, 21 recipes; ages 5–9.
- **Why selected:** the direct, no-mascot, no-brand-story competitor in Book #1's exact category — the floor Book #1 has to visibly clear, not a book to admire.
- **What to study:** how 21 recipes compress into 48–50 pages (real page-per-recipe density math); what a $13.99 mass-paperback price point signals about production-value expectations at that tier; how a zero-IP book still sequences recipes.
- **What NOT to copy:** any recipe wording, ingredient-list phrasing, or step language — flagged as the most legally/ethically sensitive comp on the list, since it's the closest direct competitor in subject matter. Structural density math is fair game; sentences are not.
- **Target reader/format:** ages 5–9, mass-market paperback.
- **Recommendation: buy a physical copy.** Cheap enough ($13.99) that a research substitute isn't worth the effort, and page-density measurement needs the physical pages.

- **What to study:** what a 16-page booklet's page count relates to at a $25–28 shelf price (i.e., what "book" means when priced as a toy) — directly informs whether Book #1 should be a pure book or a book-plus-kit; how "measure → mix → observe" instructions stay short enough for an 8-year-old.
- **What NOT to copy:** the specific experiment list or the "Slime-O-Nator" mechanic itself (a trademarked, patented physical product feature) — study the pacing convention, not the device.
- **Target reader/format:** ages 8+, boxed kit with companion booklet.
- **Recommendation: research-only is likely sufficient.** This is a $25+ purchase to see a 16-page booklet; real Amazon/YouTube unboxing reviews of this specific kit are plentiful and typically show the booklet's interior spreads page by page — a legitimate free substitute. ### 4. Who Would Win? Ultimate Showdown

- **Title / author-publisher:** *Who Would Win?: Ultimate Showdown*; Jerry Pallotta, illustrated by Rob Bolster; Scholastic Inc.
- **Details:** 2018 (5-book bind-up of the existing series: Lion vs. Tiger, Hammerhead vs. Bull Shark, Polar Bear vs. Grizzly, Hornet vs. Wasp, Triceratops vs. Spinosaurus); ISBN-13 9781338535563; $9.99 confirmed; hardcover bind-up; ages 6–9.

- **What to study:** the exact mechanism — each matchup opens with a versus-style hook, not a topic sentence — that converts a fact list into a contest; how facts get delivered inside a "who wins" frame instead of a textbook frame; page-turn pacing across a single matchup.
- **What NOT to copy:** the "who would win" matchup format itself is trademarked-in-effect as Pallotta's own long-running series identity — study the spectacle-vs-learning mechanism, not a literal "X vs. Y" framing device, which would read as a direct lift of a recognizable series.
- **Target reader/format:** ages 6–9, hardcover bind-up.
- **Recommendation: buy, or check a library first — either is fine.** $9.99, and Pallotta's series is a common school/public-library title.
