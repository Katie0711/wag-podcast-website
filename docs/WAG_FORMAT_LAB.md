# WAG Format Lab

**One executive question this module answers: what is the next signature WAG format, built from first principles rather than adapted from someone else's?**

**Permanent mission, per Katie's explicit 2026-08-09 directive:** discovering future repeatable franchises — not one-off ideas, not trends, formats that could produce dozens of great videos over years. This is deliberately separate from `WAG_VIDEO_GREENLIGHT.md` (which scores ideas *within* WAG's existing buckets) and from `WAG_PATTERN_LIBRARY.md` (which catalogs *other creators'* evidenced patterns). Format Lab's job is inventing, not adapting — the brief was explicit: "I don't want ten more versions of what we're already making. I want the next signature WAG format. Not copied. Not inspired."

**Method, stated plainly:** three independent ideation passes reasoned from raw, forensic creator mechanics (never from WAG's existing content buckets, which were deliberately withheld from the ideation stage) plus WAG's real, given constraints (three real people — two sisters, one cousin, ages 16-18 — Texas-based, no scripting ever, small team). Nine candidate formats were generated, a Curator narrowed to a 3-format shortlist, and a dedicated Red Team then attacked all three for disguised genre tropes, overclaimed novelty, and — critically — real minors-welfare risk. **This doc reports the Red Team's verdict, not the Curator's original pitch** — two of the three shortlisted formats needed real redesign, not just a caveat, before they're safe to greenlight.

---

## The verdict, upfront

Of 9 candidate formats generated, 6 were correctly killed at the Curator stage (see Rejected, below). Of the 3 that survived to the shortlist, the Red Team confirmed **one is ready to lead**, and downgraded the other two — one for a genuine minors-welfare problem, one for an internal repeatability contradiction. This is exactly the kind of honest outcome Katie asked for: *"I don't actually care if [it] gets greenlit. I care whether [the system] produces something dramatically better than the original idea."* Format Lab did — but "dramatically better" included finding a real problem before production, not just a shiny new format.

---

## Lead recommendation: SPLIT — Two Against One

**Confidence: High, with two required changes before production.**

**The container:** a single, cheap, reusable physical lock-in device (three hidden panels or levers, built once) applied to whatever real choice, disagreement, or decision the three of them are already facing that day — what to eat, who's right, whose idea wins, who does the dare. Each of the three privately locks in a pick with no visibility into the others' picks, then all three reveal simultaneously on camera. A running, dated, public tally of every 2-1 alliance across the whole series is kept and referenced on camera and in a pinned post.

**Why it's repeatable, not a one-off:** the instrument — not any single topic — is the franchise. It's content-agnostic, so it absorbs any real choice on any real day, with no format-fatigue ceiling. It also compounds instead of resetting: every episode adds one more real, dated data point to a growing alliance record (who sides with whom, how often, on what kinds of decisions) that no single episode can deliver alone.

**Why it's genuinely novel, not a reskin — the Red Team's corrected version:** the Curator's original pitch claimed the *reveal mechanic itself* was the novel piece. The Red Team correctly downgraded that claim: "simultaneous private reveal, majority vs. minority" is a common short-form mechanic already (odd-one-out, majority-rules content is all over Shorts/TikTok). **The real, defensible novelty is narrower and it's this: the season-spanning, dated, cross-referenceable alliance ledger layered on top of the reveal.** That ledger requires three same-generation people with a real, standing, multi-year relationship — a peer-coalition structure none of the five creators studied this pass (MrBeast, Ryan Trahan, Airrack, Jordan+Salish Matter, FaZe Rug) actually have. Pitch it as the ledger, not the device.

**The two required changes before production:**
1. **Reframe the pitch around the alliance ledger, not the reveal mechanic** — per the Red Team's correction above.
2. **Add a stakes floor/escalation rule.** The Red Team flagged a real repeatability risk the Curator underweighted: three close, cohabitating people may agree 3-0 far more often than they split, and "what to eat" stakes are thin. Left unaddressed, this creates real pressure to manufacture disagreement or pad episodes with trivial splits — undermining the exact "no invented premise" claim the format is sold on. Define, before launch, what makes a real day's decision "worth" a filmed SPLIT segment versus not.

**Evidence table:**

| Finding | Evidence | Source type | Confidence |
|---|---|---|---|
| A real, physically demonstrated randomizer with a genuinely unknown outcome is what makes stakes read as earned rather than staged | Ryan Trahan's Wheel of Doom: real spinning prop, real unscripted outcomes spun live at real donation thresholds | STRONG EVIDENCE (BTS profile citing production directly) | High |
| A closed-loop format needing zero new characters or stakes-selling, built on accumulated relationship history, is uniquely cheap to produce and high in emotional payoff | "Who Knows Me Better — Best Friend vs Dad" works only because the audience already holds an accumulated relationship with both parties; runtime is a lean 10:08 | VERIFIED (direct observation) | High |

**Production feasibility:** high. The only capital cost is one simple, durable, reusable lock-in prop — a single build, not a recurring expense. No scripting is possible by design (the premise depends on the three genuinely not knowing each other's picks in advance). It slots into whatever real activity WAG is already filming that day rather than requiring a dedicated shoot day.

## Parked pending redesign: The Verdict

**Confidence: downgraded by the Red Team. Do not produce until the redesign below is done.**

**The container, as originally pitched:** whenever a real, live, unresolved disagreement exists between any two of the three, the uninvolved third convenes an on-camera "trial." Both sides present real, checkable evidence (texts, receipts, timestamps, outside witnesses) within a fixed time limit; the third renders a binding verdict; the losing side completes a pre-agreed real forfeit. A running scoreboard of verdicts won/lost per person opens every episode.

**Why the Red Team downgraded it — read this before considering production:**

1. **Novelty is weaker than the Curator claimed.** "Friends put each other on trial over a real dispute" (mock-court/roommate-court content) is one of the most heavily worked tropes in relationship-based creator content — not a gap. The rotating-judge-tied-to-whichever-conflict-is-live mechanic is a real structural nuance, but it's a refinement of an existing genre, not a break from it.
2. **A real, specific minors-welfare problem — the more serious issue.** The format's own success mechanic is a permanent, public, per-person win/loss scoreboard built from **real disputes between two minors**. That's a structural welfare problem, not a topic-list problem: it creates a standing incentive to find or provoke conflict on a schedule, and it turns real interpersonal friction between minors into a scored, publicly re-referenced record — a materially higher-stakes exposure than SPLIT's preference votes or the Vault's private predictions.

**What redesign would actually take (not just a guardrail):** drop the public scoreboard; retire aired disputes from any running/re-referenced record; build in a real editorial veto and a cooling-off window before anything airs. A written "off-limits topics" list — the Curator's original proposed fix — does **not** solve this; the scoring/publication mechanic itself needs to change.

**Standing instruction from this doc:** do not greenlight The Verdict in its current form. If Katie wants to pursue a redesigned version, it needs the structural changes above evaluated and approved before any filming, not folded in as a production-day guardrail.

## Repositioned: The Vault — Sealed Predictions

**Confidence: Medium-High, repositioned as a bonus layer, not a standalone third franchise.**

**The container:** a real, physically locked container into which the three seal real, dated, written predictions, dares, letters, or bets about each other's real future, each tagged with a specific, unmovable future open-date (a birthday, a channel anniversary). Never opened early. On the fixed date, the matching capsule opens on camera and is graded against what actually happened in the intervening real time.

**Why the Red Team downgraded it — a real internal contradiction the Curator missed:** the idea-pipeline logic is sound and the elapsed-time-as-moat argument is real (a competitor cannot manufacture years of real, dated predictions between three specific real people after the fact). But the repeatability claim doesn't survive the same standard the Curator itself used to reject a different candidate, "STILL US: The Annual Rerun" — sealing is frequent and cheap, but **opening (the actual payoff) is calendar-gated to a handful of fixed dates per person per year.** That's structurally the identical "fires too rarely to sustain dozens of episodes on its own" problem that got STILL US rejected. Treating sealing and opening as one repeatable format was an inconsistency in the Curator's own reasoning.

**A second real issue, distinct from topic guardrails:** a prediction or bet a 16-year-old seals about her own future self, relationships, or choices may not be something she wants surfaced and graded on camera at 19 or 20. This needs an explicit **re-consent checkpoint at open time** — not just a pre-seal guardrail — since a minor's willingness to have something aired can genuinely change over the years between sealing and opening.

**Recommendation:** keep the mechanic, but run it as a recurring tentpole/bonus layer feeding SPLIT (the primary format) rather than a co-equal, independently repeatable third franchise.

## Rejected at the Curator stage (for the record — a system that only says yes isn't a real filter)

| Format | Why rejected |
|---|---|
| **The Line** (a fixed physical apparatus recording a beaten personal-best) | Doesn't structurally require WAG's three-person configuration — works identically for one host, two, or twenty. No relational-drama engine; escalation is just a number going up. |
| **The Carryover** (a bank-or-risk stake with a physical randomizer) | Overlaps substantially with SPLIT's group-decision content; its own spec concedes the real content engine is still "what's the risked task" each week — the exact reinventing-the-premise failure mode the whole exercise was meant to avoid. |
| **The Gauntlet** (an owned, expandable physical obstacle structure) | Requires real capex plus a minors safety-review layer its own spec has to admit. Doesn't actually require the three-person cast (rotating guests could run it identically). Duplicates The Line's recurring-apparatus mechanic while being more expensive and riskier. |
| **The Nerve Ladder** (predict-then-ask-real-strangers escalating asks) | Weakest novelty case of the nine — a well-established street-interview/social-experiment genre with an added prediction gauge, not a structural break from it. Doesn't spend WAG's actual scarce asset (the three real people's standing relationship) — it's built around one-time strangers instead. |
| **The Tally** (claims checked against evidence, forfeit on loss) | Substantially duplicates The Verdict's core mechanic but with a softer trigger — requires manufacturing claims on a schedule rather than using organically occurring real disputes, a more script-adjacent premise. |
| **STILL US: The Annual Rerun** (an identical instrument re-run yearly) | Its own spec admits the disqualifying flaw: fired once per year, it cannot sustain dozens of distinct future episodes on its own. Good as a bonus tentpole layered on a real weekly/biweekly franchise; fails as a standalone format. |

## The honest overall verdict (Red Team, verbatim reasoning preserved)

Track B did not produce a fully defensible, ready-to-greenlight format on the first pass — it produced one strong primary candidate (SPLIT) and two ideas that needed real redesign, not just a documented guardrail. All three shortlisted formats shared one weakness worth naming for future Format Lab rounds: every novelty case was benchmarked only against the 5 studied creators, not against the wider creator landscape, where majority-vote/reveal formats and mock-trial/dispute formats are both well-worn tropes. The genuinely defensible novelty in each case was narrower than first claimed. This is the system working as intended, not a failure — a Format Lab that never rejects its own shortlist isn't doing its job.
